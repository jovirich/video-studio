# Bias register

Recurring distortions in the evidence base for African history, recorded once so
they are not re-derived per source. Each source record's `critique` block should be
written with this list to hand.

These are not accusations against particular sources. They are structural properties
of how the surviving record was made, kept, and catalogued — and a researcher who
does not account for them will reproduce them faithfully while believing they are
simply following the evidence.

## Survival bias

| Pattern | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Written over oral** | Societies with a writing tradition, or with outside observers who wrote, are over-represented. Absence of documents is routinely misread as absence of history. |
| **Durable over perishable** | Stone, ceramic, and metal survive; textile, wood, leather, and organic architecture do not. Material cultures are systematically misjudged by what their climate permitted to persist. |
| **Coastal over interior** | External observers arrived by sea. The documentary record thins with distance inland, independent of what was happening there. |
| **Elite over ordinary** | Courts, treasuries, and religious institutions kept records. Most people appear only as aggregates, if at all. |
| **Conflict over continuity** | Wars, raids, and crises generate documents. Ordinary decades generate silence, which reads on a timeline as emptiness. |
| **Removed over in situ** | Objects taken to European and North American collections are catalogued, photographed, and cited; objects that remained often are not. Citation frequency tracks removal, not significance. |

## Observer bias

| Pattern | What to ask |
|---|---|
| **Colonial administrators** | Recording for taxation, control, and legitimation. Categories (tribe, chief, custom, native law) were administrative constructions that then hardened into "facts" and were sometimes adopted locally in response. |
| **Missionaries** | Recording to convert. Religious practice described as error; social structure described through the lens of what needed reform. Frequently also the most detailed ethnographic record available — useful and untrustworthy simultaneously. |
| **Traders and travellers** | Recording what bore on commerce. Interior knowledge often second-hand, from interested informants, then repeated as eyewitness. |
| **Rival polities** | Chronicles written by neighbours, victors, or successor states. Often the only source, and structurally motivated to distort. |
| **Modern nation-states** | Post-independence historiography sometimes projects national unity backward onto polities that had none, for reasons of present legitimacy. |
| **Anthropologists** | The "ethnographic present" — describing a society observed in one colonial-era decade as though it were timeless. Change was frequently invisible to the method. |

## Transmission bias

- **Translation** — through an intermediate language especially. Record every hop;
  each one is a place meaning shifted.
- **Transcription** — of oral material into writing, often by someone who did not
  speak the language natively and used an orthography that could not represent tone.
- **Selective publication** — editors chose what to print from larger corpora.
- **Archival rearrangement** — provenance and original order lost in recataloguing;
  items separated from their context.
- **Digitisation** — what got scanned reflects present institutional priorities and
  funding, and now determines what is searchable and therefore what gets cited.

## Circular sourcing

The most consequential failure in popular history and the hardest to see.

A claim appears in one nineteenth-century account. It is repeated in a colonial
gazetteer, then a mid-century survey, then a textbook, then an encyclopedia, then
every website. It now appears in five "independent" sources and reads as
overwhelmingly established.

**Trace every claim to its earliest attestation.** The `independent_of` field on
claim evidence exists for exactly this. Record the chain explicitly.

## Framing bias in our own work

Applies to this studio, not only to the sources:

- **The corrective trap.** Overclaiming grandeur to counter denigration is still
  overclaiming, and it hands critics an easy target. The answer to a distorted
  record is an accurate one.
- **The great-man frame.** Named rulers are documented; the people who made the
  polity work are not. Building narrative around the documented individual because
  the evidence is there smuggles in a theory of history.
- **The rupture frame.** Treating external contact as the organising event of a
  society's history reproduces the observer's perspective.
- **Presentism.** Reading modern national, ethnic, and religious categories back
  into periods where they did not operate as they do now.
- **Availability-driven scope.** Choosing episode subjects by where the evidence is
  easy. Legitimate as a production constraint; dangerous when it becomes an implicit
  claim about where the history was.

## Using this register

1. Read it before writing a critique block.
2. Name the specific patterns that apply, in the block. "Colonial administrator,
   recording for taxation; interior knowledge second-hand" is a useful critique.
   "Should be treated with caution" is not.
3. Where a bias is load-bearing for a claim, it frequently belongs on screen — the
   nature of the surviving record is often more interesting than the claim itself.
